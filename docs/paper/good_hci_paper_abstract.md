# What Makes a Good HCI Paper Abstract?

## Definition

A good HCI abstract is a self-contained, evidence-calibrated mini-argument. It
states a consequential human or interaction problem, identifies what is missing
from current knowledge or systems, says exactly what the paper contributes,
provides enough methodological detail to judge the evidence, reports the most
important findings concretely, and explains why those findings matter to HCI.

In one reading, a reviewer should be able to answer:

1. What human activity, experience, or interaction is at stake?
2. What specific problem or knowledge gap remains?
3. What did the authors create, study, or explain?
4. What evidence did they collect?
5. What did they find?
6. What is the paper's main contribution to HCI?
7. Are the claims appropriately limited by the evidence?

## The Core Structure

A strong HCI abstract usually contains six rhetorical moves:

### 1. Establish the consequential context

Open with the human activity or interaction problem, not a generic statement
that a technology is popular or rapidly advancing.

Weak:

> Artificial intelligence has become increasingly popular in recent years.

Stronger:

> Novice athletes practicing without a coach must recognize subtle movement
> errors while simultaneously executing a fast, coordinated action.

The stronger version identifies users, activity, and difficulty.

### 2. State the precise gap

The gap should identify what existing research, practice, or systems cannot yet
explain or support. It should be narrow enough that the paper can actually close
it.

Useful gap forms include:

- Existing systems support X, but not Y.
- Prior studies measure X, but do not explain Y.
- Practitioners need X, yet current workflows require Y.
- A technology creates a new risk, but current frameworks cover only Z.

Avoid claiming that "no research exists" unless that claim can be defended.

### 3. Name the contribution plainly

State what the paper contributes with a concrete noun:

- a system or interaction technique;
- a framework, taxonomy, or design space;
- an empirical finding or explanatory account;
- a dataset, measure, or method;
- a set of design implications grounded in evidence.

The contribution is not merely "we explore" or "we investigate." Those phrases
describe activity, not the intellectual result.

### 4. Make the evidence judgeable

Include the details that let a reader assess the scale and fit of the evidence:

- study type;
- participant or dataset size;
- relevant participant population;
- comparison conditions;
- analysis method when it is central to credibility;
- deployment duration or context when relevant.

For example, "we conducted a study" is weak. "We conducted semi-structured
interviews with 23 K-12 educators and six edtech providers" is judgeable.

### 5. Report findings, not promises

Use the abstract's most valuable space for actual results. Prefer:

- the direction of an effect;
- a meaningful comparison;
- a central qualitative relationship or tension;
- the number and nature of taxonomy categories;
- a concrete behavior, outcome, or limitation.

"The system was effective" is not enough. State what improved, compared with
what, and by how much when the magnitude is meaningful and trustworthy.

Statistical significance alone is not a result. A useful quantitative sentence
usually gives direction and practical meaning; it may also provide effect size,
confidence interval, or a compact test statistic.

### 6. End with the HCI-level meaning

The final sentence should answer "So what?" without expanding beyond the study's
evidence. It can state:

- a design implication;
- a revised understanding of users or practices;
- a limitation in current theory or technology;
- the broader class of systems to which the result applies.

The ending should generalize one level above the specific prototype, not five
levels above it.

## Four Useful Abstract Patterns

### System or interaction-technique paper

1. Activity and user problem.
2. Limitation of current tools or techniques.
3. Proposed system and its central mechanism.
4. Formative or design basis, if important.
5. Evaluation design and comparison.
6. Main performance and experience results.
7. Transferable design knowledge.

RouteFlow, rTisane, RoomDreaming, and MR.Drum all make the mechanism or design
logic visible rather than presenting a feature list.

### Qualitative paper

1. Social or technical tension.
2. Missing stakeholder knowledge.
3. Participants, recruitment scope, and method.
4. Central themes or relationship among themes.
5. Interpretation that changes how the issue should be understood.
6. Implication for design, policy, or future research.

The strongest qualitative abstracts do not list every theme. They organize the
findings around one memorable explanatory claim, such as a mismatch between
stakeholder groups or between principles and lived constraints.

### Controlled experiment

1. Consequential phenomenon and research question.
2. Conditions, sample size, and study design.
3. Primary outcome and direction.
4. Magnitude or compact statistical evidence.
5. Important null, boundary, or tradeoff.
6. Implication and risk.

Synthetic Human Memories and Debate Chatbots are effective because they report
both what changed and what did not.

### Review, taxonomy, or large-corpus analysis

1. Why current concepts or protections are insufficient.
2. Corpus and selection scope.
3. Coding or synthesis approach.
4. Main categories or organizing framework.
5. What the framework reveals that prior approaches miss.
6. Who can use the result and for what decision.

Deepfakes and the mental-health app paper make their evidence base concrete
before making broader ethical or design claims.

## Quality Criteria

### Coherence

The problem, method, findings, and contribution must describe the same paper.
If the opening promises learning outcomes but the study measures only usability,
the abstract is incoherent.

### Specificity

Replace praise words such as "novel," "effective," "intuitive," and "robust"
with mechanisms, comparisons, observations, or numbers.

### Evidence calibration

Match verbs to evidence:

- A controlled experiment may show that an intervention affected an outcome
  under tested conditions.
- Interviews may reveal, characterize, or explain participants' perspectives.
- A formative study may motivate or inform a design, but does not validate its
  effectiveness.
- A short lab study should not claim long-term behavior change.

### Contribution visibility

The reader should not need to infer why the work belongs at CHI. Name the
reusable knowledge: interaction principle, design implication, framework,
empirical relationship, or methodological lesson.

### Compression

Every sentence should perform at least one rhetorical job. Background details,
implementation components, and secondary findings should be removed unless they
are necessary to understand the contribution.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Generic importance opening:** spends words saying that AI, VR, health, or
  education is important without identifying a specific human problem.
- **Gap by adjective:** calls prior work "limited" without explaining the exact
  limitation.
- **Feature inventory:** lists system components but never states the design
  principle connecting them.
- **Method fog:** says "we evaluated the system" without sample, setting, or
  comparison.
- **Result fog:** says participants "responded positively" or results were
  "promising."
- **Significance theater:** reports p-values without effect direction or
  practical meaning.
- **Theme dumping:** lists many qualitative themes without a central
  interpretation.
- **Contribution inflation:** claims transformation of a field from a small or
  short study.
- **Missing boundary:** omits a major null result, tradeoff, or limitation needed
  to interpret the headline finding.
- **Introduction disguised as abstract:** provides extensive motivation but
  little method, result, or contribution.

## A Drafting Template

Use this as a starting structure, then revise it into natural prose:

> [Users or stakeholders] need to [activity or goal], but [specific difficulty
> or consequence]. Existing [research, tools, or practice] supports/explains
> [what it does] but not [precise gap]. We present [contribution], which
> [central mechanism or conceptual advance]. We [method] with [participants,
> data, setting, and comparison]. We found [primary result with direction and
> concrete detail], while [important secondary result, null, or boundary].
> These findings [HCI-level implication that is supported by the evidence].

For a system paper with formative and summative studies:

> [Activity] is difficult because [specific coordination, information, or
> social challenge]. To understand [stakeholder practice or unmet need], we
> conducted [formative method and sample]. These findings motivated
> [framework/design principle] and [system], which [central mechanism]. In
> [evaluation and comparison] with [sample], [system] improved/changed
> [specific outcome] and [experience finding]. This work contributes
> [transferable design knowledge] for [bounded class of HCI systems].

## Revision Checklist

Before submission, verify:

- [ ] The first two sentences identify a real human problem and a precise gap.
- [ ] The main contribution is named with a concrete noun.
- [ ] The abstract states who or what was studied and at what scale.
- [ ] The method is appropriate for the claim being made.
- [ ] At least one main result is concrete and directional.
- [ ] Quantitative claims include meaningful comparison or magnitude where
      possible.
- [ ] Qualitative findings are synthesized into an explanatory claim.
- [ ] An important null result or boundary is included when needed.
- [ ] The final sentence states reusable HCI knowledge.
- [ ] No sentence exists only to praise the work.
- [ ] Acronyms and implementation details are kept only when essential.
- [ ] Every claim is supported by the paper's actual evidence.
- [ ] The abstract can be understood without reading the title or paper.

## One-Sentence Standard

The shortest practical test is:

> A good HCI abstract lets a skeptical reader understand the human problem, the
> knowledge gap, the contribution, the evidence, the result, and its bounded
> significance without having to guess.
